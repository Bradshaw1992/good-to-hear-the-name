## Seamus Coleman
Sixty thousand pounds, seventeen years, one club. Everton plucked Coleman out of Sligo Rovers on a scout's recommendation and got the bargain of a generation, a Gaelic footballer turned overlapping right-back who simply never stopped turning up. He made the PFA Team of the Year under Roberto Martinez, climbed past Dixie Dean into Everton's all-time top ten for appearances, and captained the side in the final game ever played at Goodison Park. A Neil Taylor tackle broke his leg in two places in 2017 and FIFA paid his wages while he rebuilt it. He even managed the club for a night, alongside Leighton Baines. Eighty one caps for Ireland, and not one of them handed to him.

## Ricardo Quaresma
Nobody has ever hit a ball quite like it. The trivela, struck with the outside of the right foot and bending away from the goalkeeper, was Quaresma's signature and his argument: that there is more than one way to play. Italy gave him an award for being the worst player in the league, and the following season his club won the treble. Jose Mourinho said he had the talent but still had to learn to work for the team. He inherited Luis Figo's number seven anyway. He sat through an entire European Championship without kicking a ball, then came back four years later and scored a hundred and seventeenth minute winner on the way to Portugal lifting the thing. Maddening, magnificent, entirely himself.

## John Carew
Six foot five, Norwegian, and the first black player ever to represent his country. Carew was a problem nobody solved twice the same way: he knocked Arsenal out of Europe on two separate occasions for Valencia, won a La Liga title in Spain, and arrived at Aston Villa in a straight swap for Milan Baros. He scored his penalty in the 2001 Champions League final shootout and still went home with nothing, the first of three finals he would lose. There is a second act too, stranger than the first. He acts now, and played a jungle warrior opposite Angelina Jolie. Ninety one caps, twenty four goals, and a career that was never once boring.

## Kalvin Phillips
They called him the Yorkshire Pirlo, and Pirlo himself agreed, calling him a bit of a regista. Phillips came through at Leeds, the hometown boy in a side Bielsa dragged back to the top division, and he gave his first England shirt to the manager who made him. He started every match of a European Championship, including the final England lost on penalties, and was voted his country's player of the year. Then a forty two million pound move to Manchester City, and the harder half of the story: six hundred and forty five days between appearances, a manager apologising in public for calling him overweight, and a succession of loans. The talent was never the question.

## Roy Makaay
They called him Das Phantom because he appeared out of nowhere, and the clock agreed: ten and a bit seconds into a Champions League tie at Real Madrid, he had scored the fastest goal in the competition's history. Makaay won the European Golden Shoe at Deportivo La Coruna and remains their leading scorer of all time. He announced himself to Bayern Munich by putting a hat-trick past them, and they responded by paying a club record fee to make him theirs. For the Netherlands he was unlucky rather than unwanted, kept out by Kluivert, Bergkamp and van Nistelrooy, and he never played at a World Cup. He coaches now, including a spell at Rangers.

## Conor Coady
The quietest two-tournament career England has produced. Coady went to a European Championship and a World Cup and did not play a minute of either, yet an England assistant called him the player of the tournament at the first one, for everything he did around the squad. He was the first Wolves player to start for England since Steve Bull in 1990, and he was so reliable that a run of eighty four consecutive Premier League games ended only because of a Covid contact. Two Championship titles, won at two different clubs. He was also named Football Ally of the Year at the British LGBT Awards. He coaches England's under twenty ones now.

## Patrick Kluivert
Eighteen years old, on as a substitute, and the winner in a European Cup final. Kluivert remains the youngest player ever to score in one, and that night with Ajax was the summit rather than the start of something. What followed was six years at Barcelona that produced exactly one trophy, a Dutch goalscoring record that stood until Robin van Persie passed it, and the cruellest pattern in international football: three major tournament semi-finals in a row, two of them lost on penalties. He also played for Newcastle, which fewer people remember. He has coached almost everywhere since, from Curacao to Barcelona's academy to Indonesia.

## Thomas Gravesen
One of the strangest transfers of the modern era, and nobody saw it coming. Gravesen was a combative, shaven-headed midfielder having a perfectly good time at Everton when Real Madrid rang, and off he went to the Bernabeu to play alongside the Galacticos. Fabio Capello summed him up better than anyone, saying there would be no problems with him, he was just a little bit peculiar. He won nothing in Spain. The only trophies of his career came in Scotland, a domestic double with Celtic. He never won a single individual award. He retired at thirty two, made a fortune on investments, and now lives down the road from Nicolas Cage.

## Jens Lehmann
Every minute of an unbeaten league season, and then sent off in a European Cup final. Lehmann was Arsenal's goalkeeper through the Invincibles and a man of magnificent contradictions: eight hundred and fifty three consecutive Champions League minutes without conceding, and also the club record for red cards at Borussia Dortmund by any player, not just any keeper. He won league titles in three different countries. He saved two penalties in a World Cup shootout against Argentina using notes hidden in his sock. He sat on the bench for a World Cup final behind Oliver Kahn, and was twice voted the best goalkeeper in Europe. Never dull, never quiet.

## Diego Milito
They called him El Principe, and on one night in Madrid he played like one. Milito scored both goals in the 2010 Champions League final and was named man of the match, the centrepiece of a season that brought Inter four trophies. He had done something stranger four years earlier, putting four past Real Madrid in a single cup tie while playing for Zaragoza. Three years in Spain brought him nothing at all. He had two separate spells at Genoa before the big move came, won twenty five caps in total for a man with over two hundred club goals, and is now president of the Argentine club where he began and finished.

## Alex Oxlade-Chamberlain
Southampton's youngest ever first-team player, and for a while the most exciting teenager in England. The Ox cost Arsenal twelve million pounds at eighteen and spent seven years there collecting FA Cups and a reputation as a man who could play almost anywhere and settle nowhere. Liverpool made him a midfielder and he was flying when a knee gave way in a Champions League semi-final against Roma, costing him a year and a World Cup. He came back to win the title, and sat on the bench for the Champions League final he had helped them reach. His father played for England too. Thirty five caps, and then a late second act in Turkey and a league and cup double in Scotland, which is a better last chapter than the story looked like getting.

## Angel Di Maria
Man of the match in a Champions League final, and then the most expensive flop in British football. Di Maria left Real Madrid for Manchester United for a British record fee, lasted one season, and was gone to Paris. What makes him remarkable is what came after: he scored the winner in a Copa America final, scored in a Finalissima at Wembley, and scored in a World Cup final. Argentina spent a decade being told he was finished and he kept turning up in the games that mattered most. Over a hundred and forty caps and more than twenty goals. He went home to Rosario Central at the end, where he started.

## Antonio Valencia
Ecuador's finest export, and the only man brave enough to take the number seven shirt after Cristiano Ronaldo. Valencia came from Wigan, where he had been the best winger in the league that nobody talked about, and the shirt crushed him for a season. He handed it back, took twenty five, and became indispensable. Then Jose Mourinho turned him into a right-back and he was arguably better again, winning Manchester United's players' player award and eventually the club captaincy. Two Premier League titles, a Europa League, ninety nine caps and a World Cup for Ecuador. He finished back at Quito, where nobody needs telling who he is.

## Chris Smalling
Non-league to Old Trafford in two years. Smalling was playing for Maidstone United while studying at university when Fulham spotted him, and barely a season later Manchester United paid seven million pounds for a defender with twelve top-flight appearances. Two Premier League titles followed, and so did the criticism, including a manager telling the press in public that he and his partner were not brave enough. Rome fixed all that. He went to Roma on loan, stayed five years, won a European trophy under Jose Mourinho and was adored in a way he never quite was in England. Thirty one caps. Vegan, and happy to talk about it.

## Christian Benteke
Six foot three of Belgian nuisance, and briefly the most talked-about striker in England. Benteke arrived at Aston Villa and dragged a sinking club to safety almost on his own, which earned him a thirty two and a half million pound move to Liverpool. There he scored an overhead kick against Manchester United that was better than anything else in his season, and left after a year anyway. Crystal Palace got the long tail of his career, then Washington, where he won the MLS Golden Boot at thirty three. More than sixty caps for a golden generation, usually as the option they turned to when the clever football ran out.

## Christian Vieri
A club collector with a goal in him. Bobo Vieri played for both Milan clubs, both Turin clubs, Lazio, Atletico Madrid, Monaco and Fiorentina, among others, and never settled anywhere because somebody always paid more. Lazio sold him to Inter for a world record fee. He was the top scorer at the 1998 World Cup, got twenty three goals in forty nine games for Italy, and won precisely one Serie A title in all of it. Italy also voted him the biggest waste of talent in the league one year, which tells you how high the expectations were. Nine goals in nine World Cup matches. Nobody ever doubted the ability.

## Daniel Sturridge
The best strike partnership of the decade, and a career that could not hold itself together. Sturridge came through Manchester City, failed to break through at Chelsea, and then found Luis Suarez at Liverpool and scored twenty one league goals in a title race they lost by two points. He got England's winner against Wales at a European Championship. He also got a worldwide ban for breaching betting rules over information about his own transfer, which he always insisted was a conversation with family. Twenty six caps, a Champions League winner's medal from the bench, and the nagging sense of a player whose body never gave him a clear run.

## Danny Welbeck
Manchester United sold him and he scored the winner that knocked them out of the FA Cup. Welbeck came through at Old Trafford, won two league titles, and was moved on to Arsenal at twenty three for a fee that looked small even then. He gave Arsenal a Champions League hat-trick against Galatasaray, an FA Cup, and two serious injuries including an ankle that bent the wrong way at the Emirates. England kept picking him because the ball kept going in: sixteen goals in forty two caps, better than reputations suggest. Watford, then Brighton, where he was still scoring in the Premier League past thirty three.

## Dennis Bergkamp
The non-flying Dutchman, who would not board a plane and still became the most elegant footballer in England. Bergkamp's time at Inter was miserable and his time at Arsenal was transformative: he was the first foreign star to say yes to the club, and he arrived at a place that then won three titles and a Cup Winners' Cup, and went a whole league season unbeaten with him in the side. He once took all three places in Match of the Day's goal of the month, which no one else has done. There is a statue of him outside the Emirates and a place in the Premier League Hall of Fame. The touch, people still say, was the best there has been.

## Dida
Three saved penalties in a Champions League final shootout. Dida was AC Milan's goalkeeper across the great Ancelotti era and won two European Cups, the first on a night in Manchester where he was simply unbeatable from twelve yards. He was in Brazil's squad when they won the World Cup, without playing a minute of it. He is remembered in Britain for something else entirely: a Celtic supporter ran on and brushed past him, and he went down as though shot, which earned him a ban and a lot of jokes. Ninety one caps. Still one of the most decorated goalkeepers of the century.

## Diego Costa
Born in Brazil, capped by Brazil, and then a Spain international. Costa made the switch after two friendlies and spent the rest of his career being the most disagreeable man on any pitch he stood on, in the way his managers loved and everyone else resented. He won a La Liga title with Atletico Madrid, two Premier Leagues with Chelsea, and found out he was being sold by text message. He went back to Atletico and won the league again. There was also a short, strange spell at Wolves at the end. Twenty four goals in Spain's shirt, and roughly the same number of enemies.

## Dirk Kuyt
Not the quickest, not the most skilful, and somehow always exactly where the ball was. Kuyt scored a hat-trick against Manchester United for Liverpool in which all three goals travelled a combined few yards, which is the perfect summary of him. He played a Champions League final and a World Cup final and lost both. The Netherlands picked him a hundred and four times. The best bit came last: he went home to Feyenoord and won the league title in his final season, scoring a hat-trick on the final day to do it. One hundred per cent effort, every single week, for twenty years.

## Duncan Ferguson
A Scottish centre-forward who went to prison for something he did on a football pitch. Big Dunc headbutted an opponent while at Rangers and served three months, which is the most extreme item on a long list. He had two spells at Everton with a Newcastle detour in between, scored a famous amount with his head, and holds a share of the Premier League record for red cards. He fell out with Scotland and ended with only seven caps. Goodison adored him anyway, because he once chased two burglars out of his own house and because he always, always ran at people. He later managed the club he loved, twice, as caretaker.

## Emmanuel Eboue
Booed by his own supporters, and then applauded by them for years afterwards. Eboue had an afternoon at the Emirates where every touch was jeered, and the manager took him off to protect him. He came back from it and won two FA Cups, played in a Champions League final, and was one of the most popular players in the dressing room. Ivory Coast's golden generation used him as a right-back and sometimes a winger, and he won over a hundred caps. The hardest part came later: a Turkish title at Galatasaray, then a ban, a divorce and a public account of losing everything that he told with remarkable honesty.

## Emmanuel Petit
A blond midfielder who scored the goal that ended a World Cup final. Petit's ball into the net at the Stade de France made it three nil, and he and Patrick Vieira went back to Arsenal having won the whole thing a few weeks after winning a Premier League and FA Cup double together. Add a European Championship two years later and he had, briefly, won everything worth winning. Barcelona took him for a single poor season, Chelsea got the rest. He grew up at Monaco. His career had the shape of an arrow: Highbury for the glory, and the summer of 1998 above everything.

## Fabian Delph
He told the cameras he was not going anywhere, and was a Manchester City player within the week. Delph was Aston Villa's captain and best midfielder, and the move made him a figure of fun before it made him a champion twice over. He had the better of the deal: two Premier League titles, and a season where Pep Guardiola turned him into a left-back and it worked. England took him to a World Cup semi-final, and sent him home mid-tournament for the birth of his daughter before he flew straight back out. Twenty caps, a long injury list, and a knack for being useful in whatever role was going.

## Fabio Quagliarella
Serie A's top scorer at thirty six years old. Quagliarella scored in eleven consecutive league games that season, equalling a record held by Gabriel Batistuta, and won the Golden Boot about fifteen years later than anyone expected him to. The reason his career wandered through Udinese, Napoli, Juventus, Torino and Sampdoria is darker than restlessness: a stalker spent years sending threats to him and his family, and he left his hometown club without ever being able to explain why. The truth came out long afterwards and the apologies arrived with it. Twenty eight caps for Italy, and a late flowering that nobody begrudged him.

## Fabrizio Ravanelli
The White Feather, named for the silver hair, who scored in a Champions League final and then signed for Middlesbrough. Ravanelli won the European Cup with Juventus in 1996 and three months later was in the north east of England, where he announced himself with a hat-trick against Liverpool on his debut. That season Boro reached two cup finals, lost both, and were relegated, which may be the most Middlesbrough sequence ever recorded. He went on to Marseille, Lazio and Derby. Twenty two caps for Italy. Always complaining, always scoring, and always with the shirt pulled over his face.

## Frederic Kanoute
African Footballer of the Year, and the first winner born in Europe. Kanoute was French, chose Mali, and became the best thing to happen to Sevilla in a generation, winning two UEFA Cups and scoring against everybody. Before that he had been a West Ham and Tottenham striker who looked too languid to be as good as he was. He is remembered as much for his convictions: he covered up a betting company's logo on his shirt because his faith forbade it, and he paid for a mosque in Seville out of his own pocket when it was about to close. Thirty nine caps, and a presence larger than the statistics.

## Gabriel Agbonlahor
One club, one city, and absolutely nothing to show for it. Agbonlahor came through Aston Villa's academy and stayed almost four hundred games, finishing as their all-time leading Premier League scorer, and in all that time he won not a single trophy. He lost a League Cup final and an FA Cup final instead. He was quick in a way that Premier League defences had no answer to in the late 2000s, got three England caps during the Capello years and then was never seen again. He captained the club, got relegated with it, and retired at thirty. Villa fans still argue about whether he was underrated or whether he stopped trying. Probably both.

## Gonzalo Higuain
Three finals in three summers, and a nation that never forgave him. Higuain was a genuinely outstanding striker, the man who scored thirty six goals in a single Serie A season for Napoli to break a record that had stood since the 1920s, and the man Juventus then paid a vast fee to take off them. For Argentina he scored the quarter-final winner that took them to a World Cup final, and the missed chances in the finals that followed are what people remember. He was born in France because his father was playing there. Five La Liga titles and three Serie A titles, and a career defined by three afternoons.

## Guti
Real Madrid for seventeen years, and the most beautiful pass of the century. Guti came through the academy as a striker, became a midfielder, and spent his career as the Galactico who was not quite a Galactico, in and out of the side while collecting five league titles and three European Cups. The pass everyone remembers is a no-look backheel through a defence to set up Karim Benzema, which looks impossible every time you watch it. Spain gave him only thirteen caps, partly because of the temperament, partly because of the era. He finished at Besiktas and has coached since. Maddening, lazy, and occasionally the best footballer alive.

## Helder Postiga
Written off in England, and then he scored against England. Postiga was a Tottenham signing who managed one league goal in a season and was sent home to Porto as a failure, and within weeks he chipped a quarter-final equaliser past David James at a European Championship and ran to the camera. He had the career Spurs never saw: Porto, Sporting, Panathinaikos, Zaragoza, Valencia and Lazio, and twenty seven goals in seventy one games for Portugal. He played in two World Cups and three European Championships, and reached a final. In England he is a punchline. In Portugal he is a man who kept turning up when it counted.

## Hernan Crespo
He scored in a Champions League final for a club that did not own him. Crespo was on loan at AC Milan from Chelsea on the night in Istanbul, and his backheel flick made it three nil at half time, which remains one of the loveliest goals ever scored in a final nobody won. His career was a procession of enormous fees: a world record for a defender's wage bill when Lazio bought him from Parma, then Inter, then Chelsea twice with two Italian loans in between. Serie A's top scorer in 2001, thirty five goals in sixty four games for Argentina, and he is a coach now. Elegant and ruthless at once.

## Hulk
Named after a television programme by his own father, because of how strongly built he was. Hulk's career is the great argument for looking outside the obvious leagues: he won titles in Portugal, Russia, China and Brazil, and never played a minute in any of Europe's big five. Before any of that he spent three years in Japan, two of them in the second division, which is where he learned to hit a ball with that absurd left foot. A free kick of his won Zenit a Russian title. He played for Brazil, made his debut against England, and never played a club game in England. Sheer, unreasonable power.

## Ian Wright
Non-league at twenty one, and Arsenal's record goalscorer by thirty four. Wright was rejected as a kid, worked on building sites, and was playing Sunday football for Greenwich Borough when Crystal Palace took a chance on him. He came off the bench in an FA Cup final and scored twice. Then Arsenal, where he won a Golden Boot, a league title and a hundred and eighty five goals, a record that stood until Thierry Henry passed it. Thirty three England caps and nine goals. He is a broadcaster now, and one of the best there has been, which is a second career almost as distinguished as the first.

## Ivan Toney
A penalty taker so reliable that people stopped watching the goalkeeper. Toney came up through Newcastle's youth system without ever really playing, scored thirty one in a Championship season for Brentford, and then became the Premier League striker nobody could keep quiet. Then came an eight month ban for hundreds of breaches of betting rules, some of them on his own matches. He came back and scored a penalty in his first game. Two England caps, a huge move to Saudi Arabia, and a career with a hole blown through the middle of it that he refilled almost immediately. Nerveless is the word.

## Jaap Stam
Alex Ferguson's biggest regret, by his own admission. Stam was the best centre-half in Europe during Manchester United's treble season, immovable and quick with it, and then he wrote an autobiography, and a few months later he was sold to Lazio. Ferguson later said it was the worst decision he ever made. Stam went on to Milan and Ajax and kept being excellent, won sixty seven caps for the Netherlands, and played in a World Cup semi-final. He managed Reading to a play-off final afterwards. The strange thing is that he was never actually dropped for football reasons, not once.

## Jack Rodwell
He modelled the Great Britain kit for an Olympics and then was not picked for the squad, which is a cruelly accurate summary. Rodwell was Everton's great hope, a centre-half who could play in midfield, and Manchester City paid twelve million for him on the strength of it. He played almost nothing. At Sunderland he became a byword for the worst kind of contract, one local paper calling him the worst signing in the club's history, and his portrayal in a documentary series did not help. Three England caps. He went to Australia and captained an All Stars team against Barcelona, which is a better ending than most people realise.

## Jack Wilshere
Arsenal versus Barcelona, 2011, and for ninety minutes he was the best midfielder on the pitch. Wilshere was nineteen. The problem was never the talent, it was the ankles and the shins, and he spent the next decade having the career taken away from him a few months at a time. He still managed two FA Cups, thirty four England caps and a goal against Slovenia that lifted a whole stadium, plus the team goal against Norwich he started and finished. West Ham and Bournemouth followed, then retirement at thirty. He coaches Arsenal's youngsters now, which is where he started.

## James Ward-Prowse
The best set-piece taker England has produced since David Beckham, and the numbers say so. Ward-Prowse has scored more Premier League free kicks than anyone except Beckham, and he built an entire top-flight career on it while captaining Southampton through some grim seasons. He did it without ever winning a trophy, which is the price of loyalty at a club like that. England gave him eleven caps and no tournament. There is more to him than free kicks, including a remarkable record for never missing a game, but the free kicks are why anyone outside Hampshire knows his name. West Ham, then Nottingham Forest.

## Javier Saviola
El Conejo, the rabbit, and once the most exciting teenager in South America. Saviola was five foot six and electric, and Barcelona paid a fortune for him when he was nineteen, and then he spent six years mostly being loaned out and being almost good enough. He scored freely for River Plate before and for Benfica afterwards, which suggests the problem was Barcelona rather than him. Then he did something nobody does and joined Real Madrid, where he played even less. An Olympic gold medal, a World Youth Championship, and forty caps for Argentina. The potential was never fully cashed.

## Jermaine Beckford
He scored the goal that knocked Manchester United out of the FA Cup in the third round, the earliest Alex Ferguson had ever gone out. Beckford was a Leeds player in the third tier at the time, which made it worse. He could have played for England, Jamaica or Grenada and chose Jamaica. His one Premier League season brought goals at Anfield and against Chelsea at Goodison, and after that he dropped back down the leagues and kept scoring. He is a pundit now. His brother won a television football competition, which is the kind of detail that makes a family story rather than a career.

## Jimmy Floyd Hasselbaink
He hit a football harder than almost anyone, and he scored wherever he went. Hasselbaink won the Premier League Golden Boot at Leeds, left for Atletico Madrid and got relegated with them while being their top scorer, then came back and won another Golden Boot at Chelsea. He is one of very few men to have shared the award twice with different clubs. Born in Suriname, raised in the Netherlands, he played at a World Cup and a European Championship. Middlesbrough and Charlton came at the end. He has managed since, with mixed results, and remains a man whose shooting you simply do not forget.

## Joaquin
One of the great one-club loves, interrupted by eleven years elsewhere. Joaquin came through at Real Betis, left for Valencia and Malaga and Fiorentina, and then came home and played until he was forty one, by which time he had made more Primera Division appearances than almost anyone in history. He scored a Copa del Rey winner, lifted the trophy as captain, and became the oldest player to score a La Liga hat-trick. Fifty one caps for Spain, two World Cups, and the misfortune of being a right winger in the exact years Spain had an embarrassment of them. Always smiling, always running.

## John McGinn
Scotland's midfield for a decade, and Aston Villa's engine for longer than anyone expected. McGinn came from Hibernian, where he had won a Scottish Cup that ended a hundred and fourteen year wait, for a fee that now looks absurd. At Villa he scored in a play-off final to get them promoted, then stayed to captain them back into the Champions League. Steven Gerrard said he was the best player he had seen in training since he stopped playing with the very best. More than ninety Scotland caps and over twenty goals, and a brother who also played professionally. Everything he does looks slightly too energetic.

## John O'Shea
A full-back, a centre-half, a midfielder, and once a goalkeeper, all at Manchester United. O'Shea played thirteen years there and won five league titles, a Champions League and a Club World Cup while never being anybody's first choice in any single position, which is a remarkable trick. He scored a last-minute winner at Anfield. He kept goal in a Premier League match when both keepers were unavailable, and he kept a clean sheet in it. One hundred and eighteen caps for Ireland, then Sunderland and Reading. The nutmeg on Luis Figo is still the clip people send each other.

## Juan Pablo Angel
Aston Villa's best Colombian, and a man whose first season in England was almost unbearable. Angel arrived for a club record fee, his wife lost a baby, and he barely played. The second season he scored twenty three. He went on to be one of the most liked players in the club's modern history, and then did it all again in New York, where he was the first designated player signing that actually worked and scored freely in Major League Soccer. Thirty four caps for Colombia. River Plate before, Los Angeles and Atlanta after. Quiet, decent, and much better than the fee suggested.

## Juan Roman Riquelme
The last of the true number tens, and he refused to be anything else. Riquelme would not run back, would not press, would not move out to the wing when Louis van Gaal asked him to at Barcelona, and so he barely played there. Everywhere he was allowed to stand still and receive the ball he was sublime: two Copa Libertadores and an Intercontinental Cup with Boca Juniors, and a Villarreal side he dragged to a Champions League semi-final before missing the penalty that would have taken them to the final. Olympic gold with Argentina, fifty one caps, and a reputation in Buenos Aires that borders on the religious.

## Julio Cesar
For two or three years he was simply the best goalkeeper in the world. Julio Cesar was Inter's goalkeeper in the treble season, won a Champions League, and collected individual awards from everyone who hands them out. Brazil picked him eighty seven times. He saved two penalties in a World Cup shootout on home soil and wept openly about it, and a week later he was the goalkeeper in the seven one. In between he had a season at Queens Park Rangers that ended in relegation and which he always spoke about generously. Flamengo, Benfica, Toronto. One of football's genuinely well-liked men.

## Juninho Pernambucano
The greatest free-kick taker who has ever lived, and it is not really a debate. Juninho scored around seventy five of them, from distances that should be impossible, with a technique he worked out himself by striking the ball on the valve. He won seven consecutive league titles at Lyon and is the club's record scorer from midfield. Brazil gave him only forty caps because the competition in that era was brutal, and he played at a World Cup. Vasco da Gama before and after. Cristiano Ronaldo and Gareth Bale both studied his technique. Nobody has ever matched it.

## Jussi Jaaskelainen
Bolton Wanderers for fifteen years, more than five hundred games, and a hundred thousand pounds well spent. Jaaskelainen came from Vaasa in Finland for the kind of fee that would not buy a Premier League substitute now, and ended up as one of the best shot-stoppers in the division across an era when Bolton were in Europe and beating everybody. He saved two penalties in the same match against Blackburn, which is the clip Bolton fans still share. Then West Ham and Wigan. Fifty six caps for a Finland side that never qualified for anything, which is the only medal missing from a career of remarkable consistency.

## Kaka
The last man to win the Ballon d'Or before Messi and Ronaldo took it over for a decade. Kaka's 2007 was close to perfect: he tore the Champions League apart almost single-handedly and Milan won it. He had been a World Cup winner at twenty, a squad player who barely featured, and he was in Brazil's team for years afterwards. A swimming pool accident as a teenager fractured a vertebra and nearly ended everything before it began, which he always described as the reason he played with the shirt reading that he belongs to Jesus. The Real Madrid move never worked. Ninety two caps, and universally liked.

## Kim Kallstrom
He arrived at Arsenal with a broken back, and left with an FA Cup. Kallstrom's loan from Spartak Moscow is one of the strangest in Premier League history: the medical found a fracture, Arsenal signed him anyway because they were desperate, and he played three league games in four months before collecting a winner's medal at Wembley. The rest of the career was substantial. Two Ligue 1 titles with Lyon, spells in five different countries, and a hundred and thirty one caps for Sweden, which puts him among their most capped players of all time. A beautiful striker of a ball when his spine allowed it.

## Laurent Robert
A left foot that could do absolutely anything, attached to a man who did not fancy defending. Robert was Newcastle's winger through the Bobby Robson years and hit some of the best goals the club has seen, from free kicks and from thirty yards, and he fell out spectacularly with Graeme Souness when Souness asked him to track back. He went to Portsmouth, Benfica and eventually Australia. France capped him nine times. He is the exact type of footballer the Premier League has largely stopped signing: thrilling, selfish, and worth the ticket on his own.

## Leon Britton
Five foot four, and in 2011 statistically the best passer in the world, ahead of Xavi. Britton went to Swansea when they were in the bottom division and still there when they were beating Manchester United, which is as complete a club story as English football has produced. He made a hundred and forty one Premier League appearances and never scored once. He won a League Cup at Wembley. The supporters once passed buckets round the ground to raise the money for his loan fee and called it the Battle For Britton. They gave him the freedom of the city in the end, and nobody argued.

## Les Ferdinand
Sir Les, from Hayes in the Isthmian League to the Premier League Player of the Year. Ferdinand was a late starter who got better the longer he went on: Queens Park Rangers made him, Newcastle made him famous during the Keegan years, and Tottenham got the elegant later period, including the ten thousandth goal in Premier League history. Seventeen England caps, and no tournament to show for them. He is a director of football at the club where he began now. There are three Ferdinands in the professional game and he was the first, which is worth remembering.

## Lomana LuaLua
Seven back flips after every goal, and one of them injured him during a match against Arsenal. LuaLua was a Bobby Robson signing at Newcastle, where he was more entertainment than reliability, and the manager once told the press he should keep his mouth shut and do what he was paid for. He went to Portsmouth and then, on loan at Olympiacos, scored against Portsmouth, which was so obviously absurd that the rules were changed afterwards. Four Africa Cup of Nations and the captaincy of DR Congo, and never a World Cup. His brother and two of his cousins played professionally too, one of them Yannick Bolasie.

## Luis Garcia
He scored in every round of a Champions League they had no business winning. Garcia's goal against Chelsea in the semi-final may or may not have crossed the line, and twenty years later Liverpool and Chelsea supporters still cannot agree, which is exactly the point. He was at Anfield for three years, came from Barcelona, went to Atletico Madrid afterwards, and in that short window produced the most important goals of anyone in a red shirt. Twenty caps for Spain. The song the Kop wrote about him is still sung by people who never saw him play, which is a kind of immortality.

## Marcelo
Twenty five trophies, more than any player in Real Madrid's history. Marcelo arrived at eighteen from Fluminense and stayed fifteen years, redefining what a left-back could be and winning five Champions Leagues, the last of them as club captain. He was never a defender in the conventional sense and it did not matter, because almost nobody got past him and because the attacking was worth any risk. Fifty eight caps for Brazil, two Olympic bronzes, and the misfortune of a generation that never won a World Cup. He went home to Fluminense at the end and won the Copa Libertadores there too.

## Marco Materazzi
He scored the equaliser in a World Cup final and then got headbutted in the chest in front of the world. Materazzi's 2006 is the most famous thing about him and it should not be: he also converted a penalty in the shootout that won it. He spent a decade at Inter, winning a Champions League and five league titles, and was a defender of the old Italian school, which is to say that he fouled people. There was one season at Everton early on, which nobody remembers and he did not enjoy. Forty one caps. Perugia and Lazio before, and a great deal of noise throughout.

## Mark Noble
Seventeen years, five hundred and fifty appearances, one club, and not a single England cap. Noble was West Ham, in every sense: a local boy who captained them, got them promoted twice, and stayed through every version of the club including the move away from the ground he grew up next to. He was a proper midfielder, a reliable penalty taker, and the symbol of something the Premier League has almost run out of. The last act was a substitution brought on specifically to take a penalty against Manchester United, and he missed it, which somehow made everyone love him more. He is the sporting director now.

## Nemanja Matic
Chelsea sold him, then bought him back for roughly ten times the money. Matic went to Benfica as part of a deal for David Luiz, became one of the best midfielders in Europe there, and Chelsea paid up to correct their own mistake. He won two Premier League titles with them and scored a volley in an FA Cup semi-final at Wembley that no defensive midfielder has any business hitting. Manchester United, Roma, Rennes and Lyon followed. He retired from international football early and still finished with close to fifty caps for Serbia. Enormous, calm, and almost impossible to hurry.

## Nemanja Vidic
Half of the best central defensive partnership the Premier League has seen. Vidic came from Spartak Moscow in January, had a difficult first six months, and then spent eight years being the most uncompromising defender in England. Five league titles, a Champions League, and two Premier League Player of the Year awards, which almost no defender wins once. Serbia and Montenegro conceded one goal in their entire qualifying campaign for a World Cup with him in the side, and then he missed the tournament through injury and they were taken apart. Fifty six caps. Inter at the end. He simply did not go round people, and nobody went round him.

## Ngolo Kante
He won the Premier League, and then he won it again with somebody else. Kante was playing in the French third tier not long before Leicester City signed him, and he was the engine of the strangest title win in history, then moved to Chelsea and won the thing again the very next season. Add a World Cup, a Champions League in which he was man of the match in the final, a Europa League and a PFA Player of the Year award, and the only sensible conclusion is that he is the most quietly decorated footballer of his generation. He is famously modest, famously small, and famously everywhere.

## Nicky Butt
The forgotten one of the Class of 92, and the one who started a European Cup final. Butt only played in Barcelona because Roy Keane was suspended, and he played every minute of it, which is a good enough career highlight for anybody. Six league titles, all at Manchester United, and a treble. He scored in Eric Cantona's comeback match. Afterwards he was player of the year at Newcastle and had spells at Birmingham and Hull, and he walked out on one manager over a selection he thought was indefensible. Thirty nine England caps, and not a goal in any of them.

## Oliver Kahn
The only goalkeeper ever to win the Golden Ball at a World Cup, and he spent the final itself making the mistake everyone remembers. Kahn was Der Titan, the most intimidating presence in the Bundesliga for a decade and a half, and he won eight league titles and a Champions League with Bayern Munich, saving three penalties in the shootout in the final. Before Bayern there was Karlsruher SC. Eighty six caps, and a rivalry with Jens Lehmann that defined German goalkeeping for years. He came back as the club's chief executive, where he discovered that the job is harder from a desk.

## Oriol Romeu
A Barcelona boy who had to leave to have a career, and then got to go home. Romeu came through La Masia, could not get near the first team, and was sold to Chelsea at nineteen, where he was in the squad that won a Champions League. Southampton gave him six years and the holding role in front of the back four, where he was underrated to the point of invisibility. Then Girona played brilliantly with him in the side and Barcelona bought him back at thirty one, which almost never happens to anybody. Spain never capped him once. Not every good career needs an international record.

## Pablo Zabaleta
The warrior, and the most popular right-back Manchester City have had. Zabaleta arrived from Espanyol in the last hours of the window on the day the club was taken over, which means he saw the whole thing from the beginning: two Premier League titles, including the one decided in the final seconds of the final day, and nine years of running into people slightly harder than was necessary. Argentina picked him fifty eight times, won Olympic gold with him and lost a World Cup final with him. West Ham got the last two years. He never scored for his country, which nobody held against him.

## Pascal Gross
He scored Brighton's first ever Premier League goal, and then spent most of a decade being their best player. Gross came from Ingolstadt for a small fee and turned into the most intelligent attacking midfielder in the division that nobody outside the south coast discussed, which included a Premier League Player of the Month award in his mid thirties. He once assisted four goals in a single Bundesliga match, something only two men had managed before him. Germany did not cap him until he was thirty two, which is the most Pascal Gross fact imaginable. Nineteen caps, and a club record that took Danny Welbeck to beat.

## Patrick Bamford
Seventeen Premier League goals in a season, and one England cap to show for everything. Bamford spent his early twenties on loan from Chelsea to half the Championship without ever playing a minute for them, was voted the best player in that division, and finally found a home at Leeds, where Marcelo Bielsa made him into a genuine top-flight centre-forward. He got promoted, had the best season of his life, and then injuries arrived and took the rest of it. He is one of the game's more thoughtful figures, outspoken on mental health and on the environment, and happy to say so when it would be easier not to.

## Per Mertesacker
Six foot six, and sick with nerves before every single match. Mertesacker admitted long afterwards that he used to vomit before kick-off and spent his career dreading the thing he was best at, which makes a hundred and four caps and a World Cup winner's medal rather more impressive than they already were. At Arsenal he was the calmest man in a chaotic defence, and the story everyone tells is the FA Cup final he started having barely played all season, and won. He is the academy manager there now, which suits him exactly. The BFG, they called him, and he liked it.

## Peter Crouch
Six foot seven, and a better footballer than the joke allowed. Crouch played for eleven clubs, scored a hundred Premier League goals, and was particularly good at the thing nobody expected of a man that height, which was controlling the ball on the floor. For England he got twenty two goals in forty two games, a ratio most strikers would take, and did the robot celebration at a World Cup. He is as famous now for the podcast and the self-deprecation as for the football, which is a little unfair, because he was an excellent player for a very long time. The line about what he would be without football is immortal.

## Phil Jagielka
He once kept goal for Sheffield United in the Premier League era because he was the designated emergency goalkeeper, and he kept a clean sheet. Jagielka then became Everton's captain and one of the most reliable centre-halves in the country for a decade, got forty England caps, and scored a stoppage-time equaliser in a Merseyside derby at Anfield that is still the loudest thing most Evertonians have heard. He missed a penalty in a European Championship shootout. He won nothing at all, not one trophy in a twenty year career, which happens to good defenders at good-but-not-great clubs more often than anyone admits.

## Phil Neville
Six league titles, fifty nine England caps, and zero goals for his country. Neville was the squad man who was somehow never quite a squad man, playing full-back on either side and in midfield for Manchester United across a decade of trophies, then captaining Everton for years afterwards. The one moment that followed him around was a penalty conceded against Romania that sent England out of a European Championship. He coached, then managed England's women to a World Cup semi-final, then went to Miami. His brother and his sister were both internationals too, in two different sports, which is an unusual family.

## Raul
Real Madrid's record goalscorer for most of the modern era, and a man Atletico let slip across the city. Raul was in Atletico's academy when they shut it down, so he walked into Real Madrid instead and scored three hundred and twenty three goals for them, a record that only Cristiano Ronaldo has beaten. Six league titles and three Champions Leagues. For Spain he has a hundred and two caps and forty four goals, and not one trophy, because he belonged to the generation immediately before the good one and retired from international football just too early. Schalke and New York afterwards. He manages now.

## Raul Meireles
The mohawk, the tattoos, and a trophy collection that is quietly ridiculous. Meireles won a league title and a European trophy with Porto, moved to Liverpool and was voted their player of the year within a single season, then went to Chelsea and won a Champions League and a Europa League in two years. Add a Portuguese cup haul and a Turkish title at Fenerbahce and he has won more than most of the famous names of his era. Portugal capped him seventy six times and he reached a European Championship semi-final. He looked like a man in a band and ran like a man with a deadline.

## Robbie Fowler
They called him God on Merseyside, which tells you everything. Fowler scored a hundred and eighty three goals for Liverpool, including a hat-trick in four and a half minutes that stood as the fastest in Premier League history for over twenty years, and he was part of the treble side in 2001. England gave him only twenty six caps because Shearer, Sheringham and then Owen were in the way. Leeds, Manchester City and a brief emotional return to Anfield followed. He invested the money so well in property that his name became slang for being unexpectedly rich, which he has always found funny.

## Robert Pires
The best left foot in the Premier League for about four years, and a World Cup winner before he arrived. Pires came from Marseille with a France medal already in the bag and took a season to adjust, after which he was voted Footballer of the Year, won two league titles and three FA Cups, and was central to a side that went a whole season unbeaten. Seventy nine caps and a European Championship too. Villarreal got the late years and he turned up at Aston Villa at thirty six for a handful of games. He also took part in the most embarrassing penalty in Arsenal's history, which he has lived down.

## Santi Cazorla
He nearly lost his leg, and he came back and played for Spain again. Cazorla had an achilles tendon that became infected, needed somewhere around eight operations, and at one point the medical advice was about walking rather than football. He had already won two European Championships and two FA Cups and been the most two-footed player in England, able to cross and shoot equally well from either side, which almost nobody can genuinely do. Two years out, then back at Villarreal, then back in the national squad, aged thirty four. Eighty one caps. One of the game's most straightforwardly loved figures.

## Shaun Wright-Phillips
Released as a teenager, and an England international by twenty two. Wright-Phillips came through Manchester City's youth system after Nottingham Forest let him go, scored on his England debut, and was good enough that Chelsea paid a large fee for him, where he mostly sat on the bench and won a title anyway. Back at City he was better. Queens Park Rangers and New York followed, where he played alongside his own famous relative's legacy in a different league. Thirty six caps. His adoptive father is one of the most celebrated strikers in Arsenal's history, and his brother played for England too.

## Shola Ameobi
Fourteen years at one club, which for a striker who scored at his rate is almost inexplicable, and entirely admirable. Ameobi got seventy nine goals for Newcastle across nearly four hundred games and was simultaneously a cult hero and a running joke, which is the hardest balance in football to hold. He scored in the four four against Arsenal. He was born in Nigeria, raised in Newcastle, and played ten times for Nigeria late on. The exchange about what Jose Mourinho calls him, to which the answer was simply his own first name, is one of the great deadpan moments of the Premier League era.

## Teddy Sheringham
He came on as a substitute in a European Cup final, scored the equaliser, and set up the winner in the next ninety seconds. Sheringham's treble season with Manchester United also included scoring in an FA Cup final, which is an absurd three weeks of work. Before that he had been Tottenham's best player for years and formed the England partnership with Alan Shearer that got them to a European Championship semi-final at home. Fifty one caps, eleven goals. He played in the Premier League until he was forty, which was a record for an outfield player, and he never looked like he was running.

## Theo Walcott
Picked for a World Cup squad at seventeen without having played a single top-flight match. Walcott was Southampton's youngest ever player, cost Arsenal five million pounds as a schoolboy, and then spent twelve years at the club being both electrifying and slightly unsatisfying, which is the Arsenal condition. He scored a hat-trick for England in Croatia that remains one of the best away performances anyone has produced in that shirt, and still only got forty seven caps. Three FA Cups. Everton, then back to Southampton to finish where he started. The pace was real, and the chances it created never quite added up.

## Thiago Motta
Brazilian by birth, Italian by international career, and now better known for a clipboard. Motta came through Barcelona's academy, won a Champions League there, then injuries pushed him to Atletico Madrid and Genoa before Inter made him a holding midfielder of real distinction and he won the treble. Paris Saint-Germain gave him five league titles. He played thirty times for Italy. Then he became a coach, invented a system he described with numbers that made no sense to anybody, took Bologna into the Champions League on a fraction of the money, and discovered at a much bigger club how quickly that goodwill evaporates.

## Victor Valdes
Five Zamora trophies, three Champions Leagues, and almost nobody outside Catalonia rated him. Valdes was Barcelona's goalkeeper for the entire Guardiola era, kept more clean sheets than anyone in the club's history, and was constantly described as a weakness by people who had not watched him sweep up behind that high line. Spain capped him twenty times, and he was in the squads that won a World Cup and a European Championship without playing. Then a strange, short Manchester United spell that ended in a public row with the manager, and Standard Liege and Middlesbrough after that.
